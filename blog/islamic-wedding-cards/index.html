Islamic wedding cards: design ideas, symbols and etiquette

Islamic wedding cards are often among the most beautiful in India, drawing on centuries of calligraphy, geometry and architecture. Here is how to choose a design that is elegant, respectful and right for your family.

Islamic art has a distinctive approach to beauty. It celebrates the written word, the infinite repetition of geometry and the shapes of gardens and arches, and it traditionally avoids depicting people and animals in religious contexts. Muslim wedding cards in India draw on this heritage, from Mughal and Awadhi styles to Hyderabadi and Kerala Mappila traditions. This guide covers the choices you will make when designing or choosing Muslim wedding invitation cards.

For the words themselves, see our companion guide to Muslim wedding invitation wording.

Common elements of Islamic wedding invitation cards

  • Bismillah at the top, as calligraphy, in translation or as 786.
  • Calligraphy of the couple's names in Arabic or Urdu script alongside English.
  • Geometric patterns: stars, interlaced lines and tessellations.
  • Arches and domes: the mihrab-like arch is a favourite frame for names.
  • Jaali: carved stone lattice screens from Mughal architecture.
  • Crescent and stars: a widely recognised motif, used gently.
  • Gardens: the charbagh, cypress trees, roses and flowing water.

Colours

Emerald green, deep teal, ivory and gold are classic choices. Navy with gold feels regal for an evening Walima. Blush and sage suit a daytime Nikah. Whichever palette you choose, make sure event details are printed in a colour dark enough to read in low light, since many guests will check the card on the way to an evening venue.

A card set for every function

Many Muslim weddings send a set rather than a single card:

  1. The main card, from the bride's family, for the Nikah.
  2. A Mehndi or Manjha insert, more colourful and playful.
  3. A Walima card, often sent separately by the groom's family.
  4. A small map or directions card for out-of-town guests.

Digital invitations simplify this. One link can hold every function, each with its own date, time, venue and map, and the groom's family can share the same link for the Walima.

Respectful use of sacred text

Many families feel strongly about where sacred words appear. A few practical guidelines:

  • Place Bismillah and any verse at the very top, never overlapped by images.
  • Quote verses accurately, ideally with the surah and ayah reference and a trusted translation.
  • Some families prefer "786" or an English translation on printed cards that might be discarded, and keep Arabic text for the digital version.
  • Avoid placing sacred text on envelopes, packaging or sweet boxes.
  • When in doubt, ask your local imam or a respected elder.

Avoiding figures in the artwork

Families have different views on images of people. Many prefer cards without human or animal figures, especially near sacred text. Architecture, gardens, florals, geometry and still life give you a rich range of designs without any figures at all. If you want a couple illustration for a Mehndi insert, consider keeping it separate from the main card that carries Bismillah.

Calligraphy and typography tips

  • Use a proper Arabic or Urdu font with correct letter joining, and check that text flows right to left.
  • Keep calligraphy for the names or Bismillah, and use a clear font for dates and venues.
  • Leave generous space around calligraphy; crowding it with ornaments reduces its beauty.
  • If the couple's names are in both Urdu and English, keep them the same visual weight so neither feels secondary.

Frequently asked questions

What should be on an Islamic wedding invitation card?

Bismillah or its translation, the hosts, the couple's names, the Nikah and other functions with dates, times and venues, and a closing request for du'as. Design often features calligraphy, geometry, arches or gardens.

Can Islamic wedding cards have pictures of the couple?

Some families are comfortable with photos or illustrations of the couple, while many prefer to avoid figures, especially near sacred text. Follow your family's view and keep any images separate from Bismillah and verses.

What colours are used on Muslim wedding cards?

Emerald green, teal, ivory, navy and gold are popular, along with soft pastels for daytime functions.

Are digital Muslim wedding invitations acceptable?

Yes, they are widely used. Some families prefer them because sacred text is not printed on paper that might be thrown away.
