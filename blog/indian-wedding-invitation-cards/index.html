Indian wedding invitation cards: a complete guide to styles, wording and etiquette

From a Ganesha card in Jaipur to a Prajapati butterfly in Kolkata, Indian wedding invitation cards are as diverse as the country. This guide brings everything together so you can choose with confidence.

An Indian wedding invitation is more than a piece of paper or a link. It is often the first formal act of the wedding: families offer the first card to their deity, elders deliver cards in person, and the design sets the tone for every function that follows. This guide covers what goes into Indian wedding invitation cards, the styles by region and faith, the formats available today and the etiquette that keeps everyone happy.

What every Indian wedding card includes

Whatever your tradition, a good invitation answers the questions guests will ask:

  1. Who is inviting: the hosts, usually parents or both families.
  2. Who is getting married: the couple, often with their parents' names.
  3. What, when and where: each function with day, date, time and venue.
  4. Any auspicious time: a muhurat, muhurtham or lagna if your family follows one.
  5. How to respond: an RSVP contact and date.
  6. A blessing and a closing line, according to your faith and family.

Styles by region and language

India's regional traditions shape both wording and design. Our detailed guides cover each one:

  • Hindi and North Indian: Ganesha, kalash and red and gold. See Hindi wedding invitation card wording and the shadi card guide.
  • Marathi: Paithani borders and precise muhurta times. See the Marathi wedding invitation card.
  • Gujarati: the kankotri, bandhani and garba. See the Gujarati wedding invitation card.
  • Punjabi: phulkari and Anand Karaj. See Punjabi wedding invitation cards.
  • Bengali: the Prajapati butterfly and alpana. See the Bengali wedding invitation card.
  • Tamil, Telugu and Kannada: kolam, kuthuvilakku and the muhurtham. See Tamil, Telugu and Kannada invitations.

Styles by faith

  • Hindu: an invocation such as Shri Ganeshaya Namah, with the muhurat and functions like haldi, mehendi and pheras.
  • Sikh: Ik Onkar and the Anand Karaj at the gurdwara.
  • Muslim: Bismillah, the Nikah and the Walima. See Muslim wedding invitation wording and Islamic wedding cards.
  • Christian: a Bible verse and the church ceremony. See Christian wedding invitations.
  • Buddhist: the Triple Gem and a vihara ceremony. See Buddhist wedding invitation cards.

Interfaith and intercultural couples often combine elements from both families, for example a Ganesha blessing beside a Bible verse, or English for event details with each family's language for blessings.

Etiquette and traditions

  • The first card is often offered to the family deity or at a temple, gurdwara, church or dargah, according to faith.
  • Elders and close relatives usually receive their card in person, often with sweets.
  • Address cards correctly: "and family" signals everyone in the household is invited; just names means only those people.
  • Follow up digital invites with a call for elders who may not check messages often.
  • Send reminders two or three days before each function, with the venue map.

A timeline that works

  • 6 months before: send save-the-dates for destination or outstation weddings. See wedding save the dates.
  • 3 to 4 months before: finalise guest list, wording and design.
  • 10 to 12 weeks before: print or prepare digital invites.
  • 6 to 8 weeks before: distribute cards and send digital invitations.
  • The final week: send reminders and maps.

Saving money without losing charm

  1. Print fewer, better cards for elders and use digital invites for friends and colleagues.
  2. Combine inserts into one card with a clear schedule.
  3. Choose standard sizes that do not need custom envelopes.
  4. Proofread thoroughly so you never pay for a reprint. Our wedding card editing guide has a checklist.

Who receives which card

Many families send different versions to different guests. Elders and close relatives receive a printed card in person, often with sweets. Extended family and family friends receive a printed or digital card. Friends and colleagues receive a digital invite, sometimes a more casual version written by the couple. Keeping a simple spreadsheet with a column for each version prevents anyone from being missed.

Frequently asked questions

What should be written on an Indian wedding invitation card?

The hosts, the couple and their parents, every function with day, date, time and venue, any auspicious time, an RSVP contact and a blessing or closing line suited to your faith and family.

How early should Indian wedding cards be sent?

Six to eight weeks before the first function is common, with save-the-dates three to six months ahead for destination or outstation weddings.

Are digital wedding invitations acceptable in India?

Yes. Digital invitations are widely accepted, especially for friends, colleagues and guests abroad. Many families still give printed cards to elders.

What are the most popular Indian wedding card designs?

Classic red and gold with Ganesha, royal Rajasthani palaces, floral pastels, minimal modern designs and regional motifs such as phulkari, kolam and the Prajapati butterfly.
